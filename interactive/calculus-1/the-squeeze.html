The Squeeze Theorem

Trapped between two curves

Does x^2\sin\!\left(\tfrac1x\right) have a limit as x\to 0? The sine factor swings between -1 and 1 faster and faster near 0. You cannot factor it, cancel it, or substitute. What else could pin the limit down?

The sine never leaves [-1,1], so the function is trapped between two simpler curves, -x^2 and x^2. Shrink the ochre window around a and watch the corridor the function is forced to stay inside.

The squeeze theorem

Suppose that for all x near a, except possibly at a itself,

g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=\lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L.

Then \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L.

Why should that be true? Pick any \varepsilon>0. Close enough to a, both g(x) and h(x) are within \varepsilon of L. Since f(x) sits between them, it is within \varepsilon of L as well. That is the corridor in the graph closing up.

Both conditions matter. Turn off "Show f" in the graph. Can you still say what the limit is? Now choose \sin(1/x). Its bounds do not meet. What can you conclude then?

The limit of sin x / x

Substituting gives \tfrac00, and there is nothing to factor. Comparing areas in the unit circle shows that for 0<|x|<\tfrac{\pi}{2},

\cos x\le\frac{\sin x}{x}\le 1.

Both bounds approach 1 as x\to 0, so the squeeze theorem gives \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1. This limit is the reason the derivative of \sin x is \cos x.

    Common mistakes

    Multiplying an inequality by x

    For x<0, multiplying -1\le\cos(1/x)\le 1 by x reverses the inequalities. Multiply by |x|, or bound the absolute value instead.

    Bounds that do not meet

    Knowing -1\le\sin(1/x)\le 1 traps the function in a corridor of height 2 that never closes. The squeeze theorem then says nothing.

    Saying sin(1/x) approaches 0

    As x\to 0, 1/x grows without bound, and \sin(1/x) keeps swinging between -1 and 1. It is the factor x^2 that drives x^2\sin(1/x) to 0.
