Continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem

    The definition

    A function f is continuous at a when

    \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a).

    That one equation packs in three conditions: f(a) is defined, the limit exists, and the two are equal. Each kind of discontinuity breaks a different one.

    Removable

    The limit exists, but f(a) is missing or has the wrong value. Redefining one point fixes it.

    Jump

    The one-sided limits exist but differ, so the limit does not exist. No single value at a can fix it.

    Infinite

    At least one side grows without bound, usually at a vertical asymptote.

    Polynomials, rational functions, roots, and the trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions are continuous at every point of their domains. Sums, products, quotients and compositions of continuous functions are continuous too, where they are defined. That is why direct substitution works so often.

    The Intermediate Value Theorem

    Suppose f is continuous on [a,b] and N is any number between f(a) and f(b). Then there is at least one c in (a,b) with f(c)=N.

    The theorem says a root exists without telling you where. Bisection turns it into a method: check the sign at the midpoint and keep the half where the sign changes. Each step halves the interval that must contain the root. How many steps would it take to pin a root down to three decimal places?

      Common mistakes

      Checking only that f(a) exists

      A function can be defined at a and still be discontinuous there, as with a jump or a misplaced point.

      Using the IVT without continuity

      \tfrac1x is negative at -1 and positive at 1, yet it is never 0. The theorem needs continuity on the whole closed interval. Choose it in the bisection graph and watch where bisection ends up.

      Reading the IVT backwards

      If f(a) and f(b) have the same sign, the theorem guarantees nothing. There may still be roots in between, an even number of them.
