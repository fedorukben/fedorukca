Why the conjugate works

For any two terms, (A-B)(A+B)=A^2-B^2. If A is a square root, then A^2 has no square root left. The expression A+B is called the conjugate of A-B.

Multiplying the top and bottom by the same conjugate is multiplying by 1, so the value does not change:

\frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2}=\frac{x-4}{(x-4)(\sqrt{x}+2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2}\qquad\text{for } x\ne 4

Now the factor x-4 appears in both the numerator and the denominator, and it cancels exactly as it did when factoring. The simplified function is continuous at 4, so the limit is \tfrac{1}{\sqrt4+2}=\tfrac14.

Notice that x-4=(\sqrt{x}-2)(\sqrt{x}+2) for x\ge 0. Rationalizing is factoring in disguise. Why is it usually best to leave \sqrt{x}+2 unexpanded in the denominator?

    Common mistakes

    Multiplying only the numerator

    Multiplying the numerator alone by \sqrt{x}+2 changes the value of the expression. Multiply the top and the bottom, so you are multiplying by 1.

    Splitting a root across a sum

    \sqrt{x+5} is not \sqrt{x}+\sqrt{5}. Test it at x=4: the left side is 3, the right side is about 4.24.

    Losing a sign

    In (2-\sqrt{x+3})(2+\sqrt{x+3})=4-(x+3)=1-x, the result is -(x-1). After cancelling, a factor of -1 remains.

    Rationalizing when it is not needed

    If substitution gives an ordinary number, that number is the limit. If it gives \tfrac{k}{0} with k\ne0, no algebra will produce a finite limit.
